Musical instruments

Flutes 40,000 years old have been found, carved from vulture bones. Before writing, before farming, humans were already making music. It isn't a luxury: it's one of the oldest things we do together.

Where a sound comes from

A sound is a vibration that moves the air. An instrument always has two parts: something that vibrates and something that amplifies (a body, a tube, a skin).

The pitch of a note depends on the speed of vibration, in vibrations per second (hertz). The tuning A vibrates at 440 Hz. Doubling the frequency gives the same note, an octave higher.

For a string, three things raise the note:

  • shortening it (that's what a finger does on a guitar neck);
  • tightening it;
  • making it lighter (a thin string sounds higher than a thick one).

For a tube, its length sets the note: the shorter, the higher. The holes of a flute shorten the tube.

The five families

Museums sort every instrument in the world into five families, according to what vibrates.

IdiophonesThe instrument itselfXylophone, balafon, bell, triangle, steel drum, maracas

Instruments that made history

  • The bone flute, 40,000 years, Germany: the oldest known instrument.
  • The lyre and the harp, Mesopotamia, about 4,500 years ago (the lyres of Ur).
  • The drum, on every continent, for thousands of years.
  • The organ, born in Greece with the hydraulis in the 3rd century BC, king of Europe's churches in the Middle Ages.
  • The oud, Middle East, ancestor of the lute and so of the guitar.
  • The sitar, India, with strings that ring on their own in sympathy.
  • The kora, West Africa, 21 strings, the instrument of the griots.
  • The violin, Italy, 16th century; the luthiers of Cremona (Stradivari, Guarneri) fixed it for good.
  • The piano, Bartolomeo Cristofori, Florence, around 1700: the first keyboard that plays loud or soft depending on the strength of the finger.
  • The accordion and the harmonica, 19th century: metal reeds, a whole instrument in a pocket.
  • The saxophone, Adolphe Sax, Belgium, 1846.
  • The steel drum, Trinidad, 1930s and 1940s: made from abandoned oil drums.
  • The electric guitar, 1930s to 1950s: a magnetic pickup reads the string, an amplifier does the rest.
  • The synthesizer, Robert Moog, 1964: sound made entirely by circuits.

Making one with what you have

The steel drum was born from oil drums, the blues from one-string guitars nailed to a board. Almost anything can sound.

  • A whistle: a hollowed piece of reed or elder, a bevelled notch, a wooden plug leaving a slit. Each hole you drill adds a note.
  • A one-string bass: an upturned tub, a broom handle resting on its edge, a string stretched from the handle to the centre of the tub. Push the handle to tighten the string and change the note.
  • A box guitar: a wooden box or a can for the body, a plank for the neck, strings of fishing line, bike brake cable or thin wire.
  • Percussion: drums, pans, bottles filled with water to different levels (the more water, the higher when tapped).
  • A drum: a skin or an inner tube stretched over a bucket, tightened with a cord.

Musicians' tips

  • Tuning without a tuner: one reference note is enough (a whistle, a bottle; in France, the landline dial tone is an A at 440 Hz). The other strings are tuned to each other by ear, comparing two strings that should play the same note: when the sound "beats" (wobbles), they're off; when it becomes smooth, it's right.
  • Strings wear and sound duller and duller. Wipe them with a dry cloth after each use; boiling them brings them back a little, an old bass player's trick.
  • Wood moves with humidity. A wooden instrument is kept away from a stove and a damp wall. A guitar that cracks is almost always a heater that's too close.
  • One instrument per group is enough to sing together. Music makes time pass, calms children, marks celebrations and mourning. See Tenir dans la durée.
