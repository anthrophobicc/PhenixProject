Trading, negotiating, keeping your word

Axis 3 · Survival · Social psychology and barter

TimeShort
ContextTwo people
RiskExposed
GearNone

When things break down, your reputation travels faster than you do. It's worth more than anything you carry.

UNDERSTAND

A trade only happens if each side values the same thing differently. That's the only rule, and everything follows from it: value isn't in the object, it's in the other person's need. A lighter is worth a day's work to someone with no fire, and nothing to someone who has three.

Three properties make a good trading good. It must be divisible, so the value can be adjusted; durable, so it doesn't lose value while you wait; and widely wanted, so you don't depend on a single buyer. Salt, medicine, ammunition, batteries, blades, fuel and alcohol tick all three boxes in almost every situation on record. Salt owes part of its value to what it makes possible: see Preserving food.

But the most tradable good isn't an object: it's a skill. It doesn't run out, can't be stolen, doesn't need carrying, and it makes you valuable rather than rich. Someone who can treat a wound — see Severe external bleeding —, repair or grow food doesn't need to be defended by force: they're protected by their usefulness.

On negotiation itself, two mechanisms work everywhere.

Whoever needs the deal least gets the best price. It's mechanical, and it has nothing to do with skill. The practical consequence is unpleasant but clear: never let your need show, and never negotiate for water on the day you've run out. Planning ahead is itself a negotiating move.

The first number anchors everything after it. Once an order of magnitude is on the table, the discussion revolves around it, even if the number was absurd. That means thinking about what you announce before you say it, and being wary of your own reaction when the other side names a figure first.

And there's a mechanism deeper than those two. In a group whose members keep meeting again, cooperation beats predation, because the predator loses all future trades. That's why keeping your word isn't decorative morality: it's the strategy that pays best as soon as you're likely to meet again. It's also why a passing stranger is structurally more dangerous than a neighbour — they have nothing to lose.

ACT

  1. Meet in open ground, by day, away from your supplies. Never negotiate at home. What the other person sees, they'll remember.
  2. Show one item, never a stock. Quantity changes the nature of the relationship: it turns a partner into a target.
  3. Listen before you offer. What the other person is looking for tells you what they lack, and what they lack sets the price far better than your own estimate.
  4. Start with a partial trade. A small successful deal builds the trust a big one needs. A small failed deal costs little.
  5. Close the deal clearly and out loud. What is traded, for what, and when. Misunderstanding causes more conflict than bad faith.
  6. Keep your promises, even when they've turned against you. It's the only investment whose return grows over time.

ADAPT

You're alone facing a group. It's the situation How societies collapse describes at the scale of whole societies: with no margin, the smallest shock becomes decisive. The balance of power is against you and everyone knows it. Don't try to hide it: lower the stakes. A small, quick, polite trade, without showing what you're carrying. And agree on a next meeting — an ongoing relationship protects you better than a negotiation you've won.

You're offered far too much for what you give. It's the most reliable warning sign there is. Either the other person knows something you don't about the real value, or the payment will never come.

You're negotiating for a group. One person speaks, chosen in advance, with a limit set in advance. A group that negotiates with several voices shows its disagreements, and a visible disagreement is something to grab onto.

Trust is broken. Switch to simultaneous exchange: each side puts down, each side takes, at the same time, in the open. It's slower and less pleasant, and it lets you trade with someone you don't trust — which is very often the only choice.

You have a rare skill. Don't sell it by the job, trade it for a place. Treating someone once earns a meal; being the one who treats people earns a roof, protection, and a reason for the group to keep you.
