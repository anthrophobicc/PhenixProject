## What's inside

A tyre isn't a block of rubber. It's an assembly of layers, each with a job.

- **The tread**: the rubber that touches the ground, with grooves that clear water away.
- **The belt**: two layers of crossed steel cords under the tread. They stiffen it and keep it flat.
- **The carcass**: textile cords (polyester, rayon) embedded in rubber, running from one edge to the other. It's the tyre's frame.
- **The sidewalls**: the sides, flexible, soaking up shocks. They're thin: this is the fragile part.
- **The beads**: two rings of braided steel wire that press the tyre onto the rim and stop it jumping off.
- **The inner liner**: a layer of butyl rubber, almost airtight. It's what replaces the inner tube in a "tubeless" tyre.

## Radial or bias-ply

In a **radial tyre**, the carcass cords run straight from one bead to the other, like spokes, and the steel belt holds the tread. The sidewall flexes, the tread stays flat: better grip, less heat, longer life. Michelin patented it in 1946 and sold it from the end of the 1940s; today it's on almost every car.

In a **bias-ply tyre**, older, the cords cross diagonally. It's still used on some farm machinery, trailers and classic motorbikes.

## The rubber

- **Natural rubber**, tapped from the rubber tree, and synthetic rubbers made from oil.
- **Carbon black**: this is what makes tyres black. It strengthens them and protects them from ultraviolet.
- **Silica**, in recent tyres, which improves wet grip and lowers rolling resistance.
- **Sulphur**: heated with the rubber, it links its molecules together. That's **vulcanisation**, discovered by Charles Goodyear in 1839. Without it, rubber gets sticky in the heat and brittle in the cold.

## Making one

The layers are built up flat on a drum, forming a soft "green" tyre. It then goes into a heated press, around 150 to 180 °C: a mould stamps its tread pattern and markings while the rubber vulcanises, in about ten minutes for a car tyre.

## Reading a sidewall

Example: **205/55 R16 91V**

| Marking | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 205 | Width, in millimetres |
| 55 | Sidewall height, as a percentage of the width |
| R | Radial |
| 16 | Rim diameter, in inches |
| 91 | Load index: 615 kg (1,356 lb) per tyre |
| V | Speed rating: 240 km/h (149 mph) maximum |

**The date of manufacture**: a four-digit code in an oval, after the letters DOT. **2319** means the 23rd week of 2019.

**Winter tyres** carry the symbol of a three-peaked mountain with a snowflake (3PMSF). The M+S marking alone means "mud and snow", without guaranteeing real performance on snow.

## What wears it and what kills it

- **Under-inflation**: the sidewall flexes too much on every turn, heats up and eventually bursts. It's the leading cause of tyre destruction. The recommended pressure is on a label in the door frame or the fuel flap, and is measured **cold**.
- **Age**: even with little use, rubber hardens and cracks. Cracks on the sidewalls or between the tread blocks: it's time to replace it, whatever its depth. Generally count 6 to 10 years at most.
- **Wear**: European law requires at least **1.6 mm** of tread. Wear indicators, small bars at the bottom of the grooves, come level at that depth.
- **Driving on a flat**, even for a few hundred metres, destroys the inside of the sidewall. See [[SURV-REC-024]].

## Tips from people who fit them every day

- **A one-euro coin** slipped into a groove: if its gold rim shows, there's more than about 3 mm left. Less, and the tyre is tired on wet roads. (In the US, the same test uses a penny and Lincoln's head.)
- **A tyre that loses a little air every week** often has a nail in the tread, or a corroded rim leaking at the bead. Soapy water bubbles where it leaks.
- **Aircraft tyres** are inflated with nitrogen: no moisture, no oxygen, no fire if they overheat.
- **An old tyre is a resource**: sandal soles (Vietnamese soldiers walked on them), earth-filled retaining walls, protection, bumpers. **Burned, it gives off toxic black smoke** and oils that pollute the soil: never use it as fuel.
