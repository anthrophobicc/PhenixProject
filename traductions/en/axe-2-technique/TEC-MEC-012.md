::A train has no steering wheel. It turns because its wheels are slightly conical, and it takes curves because its wheels sit on carriages that pivot under the body: the bogies.::

## What it is

A **bogie** (called a *truck* in North America) is a carriage with two axles, sometimes three, placed under each end of a coach or locomotive. The body rests on it through a pivot: **the bogie can turn under it**. That's what lets a twenty-metre vehicle follow a curve, while its wheels stay parallel to the rails.

A freight wagon or passenger coach has two bogies, so four axles and eight wheels. A bogie weighs several tonnes.

## The wheelset: why a train turns without steering

**The two wheels of an axle are fixed to the same shaft.** They always turn together, at the same speed. In a curve, the outer wheel has further to travel: how does it manage?

**The tread is conical.** When the wheelset shifts towards the outside of the curve, the outer wheel rolls on a larger diameter and the inner wheel on a smaller one. The outer wheel then covers more ground per turn, and the wheelset centres itself. **That's what guides the train**, far more than the flanges.

**The flange**, the rim on the inside of each wheel, is only a safety: it should only touch the rail in tight curves and at switches. When it rubs, you hear the high screech of trains in curves.

## The suspensions

- **Primary suspension**, between the axle boxes and the bogie frame: metal springs or rubber blocks. It absorbs track defects.
- **Secondary suspension**, between the bogie and the body: often air springs on passenger trains. That's what makes the ride comfortable.

## What goes on a bogie

- **Brakes**: discs on the axle, or blocks rubbing on the tread.
- **Traction motors**, on the powered bogies of locomotives and multiple units. The others are called trailer bogies.
- **Sensors** for speed and axle box temperature, and the beacons of the speed control system.

## Variants

- **The Jacobs bogie** is shared between two cars, at their joint. It's the principle of articulated trainsets like the TGV: fewer bogies, a more stable and lighter train.
- **Variable-gauge bogies** change the distance between their wheels while rolling slowly through a special installation, to pass from one network to another, between Spain and France for example.
- **Trams and metros** have smaller bogies for much tighter curves.

## Tips from people who maintain them

- **Hunting**: above a certain speed, a conical wheelset swings left and right more and more. The whole design of a high-speed bogie aims to damp it. A worn bogie that "hunts" is felt in the body as a regular sway.
- **Wheels are reprofiled** without being removed: the train rolls over an underfloor lathe that shaves the tread back to its conical profile.
- **A "flat" on a wheel**, made when the wheel skidded locked under braking, makes a regular hammering noise: the famous "clack-clack-clack" of a train arriving with a damaged wheel.
- **Axle boxes** heat up when their bearing is dying. Trackside detectors measure their temperature as trains pass, and stop the train before the axle breaks.
