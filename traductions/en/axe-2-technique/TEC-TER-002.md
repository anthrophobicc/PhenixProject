## The French doctrine: hit fast

France's strategy rests on the **massive attack on new fires**: during the risk season, resources are pre-positioned and aircraft patrol loaded with water, to hit every new fire within minutes. **The vast majority of fires are stopped this way before they cover a single hectare.** A fire that escapes that first attack, on the other hand, can last for days.

## Who responds

- **Firefighters** from the departmental fire services, in wildland fire tenders, grouped into columns.
- **Civil Security military units**, specialised in large fires and reinforcements.
- **Foresters**, who know the ground, the tracks and the water points.
- **Air assets**: **Canadairs**, which scoop about 6 tonnes of water in a dozen seconds skimming the sea or a lake, **Dash 8** aircraft, which drop a red retardant, and water-bombing helicopters.

## Direct attack

When the fire is still small or calm, it's attacked **on its edge**, where the flames meet the vegetation: water from hoses, fire beaters, shovels, thrown earth. **Always starting from a safe point**, a road, a rock, an already burned area, called **the anchor point**, and working up the **flanks** towards the head. A violent fire head is never attacked from the front.

## Indirect attack

When the fire is too strong, crews work **ahead of it**, at a distance:

- **The control line**: a strip where all fuel is removed down to bare soil, by bulldozer or hand tools, tied into a road or track.
- **Retardant** dropped from the air: a product based on ammonium salts that soaks the vegetation and slows burning, even after the water has evaporated.
- **Tactical fire**, or backfire: deliberately lighting, from a control line, a fire that burns the vegetation **towards** the wildfire. When the front arrives, it finds nothing left to burn. A powerful and dangerous technique, reserved for trained personnel and precise wind conditions.

## Night and mop-up

- **At night the fire calms down**: the air cools and grows damper, the wind often drops. That's when ground crews gain the most ground, while aircraft aren't flying.
- **A contained fire isn't out.** The edge must be **mopped up**: digging, turning, soaking every stump and root that smokes, tens of metres inside the burned area. A poorly mopped-up fire restarts the next day with the afternoon wind.

## Crew safety

Wildland firefighters all over the world apply the same rule, summed up in the United States by the acronym **LACES**:

- **Lookouts**: people watching how the fire develops;
- **Anchor points**: a safe anchor;
- **Communications**: a radio that works;
- **Escape routes**: escape routes scouted in advance;
- **Safety zones**: safe areas to shelter in, often an already burned area.

The oldest rule of ground crews fits in one image: **keep one foot in the black**, meaning stay at the edge of the already burned area, where the fire can't come back.

## What the old hands know

- **Fire changes in the early afternoon**: the hottest, driest, often windiest hour. Many tragedies happen between 2 and 5 pm.
- **A sudden lull is suspicious**: the wind can shift and turn a quiet flank into a fire head.
- **Narrow gullies, steep ravines and saddles** act like chimneys: fire speeds up violently there. Never go into one above a fire.
- **Aircraft don't fly at night or in thick smoke**, and wind that's too strong grounds them: the most dangerous days are often the ones where they help least.
