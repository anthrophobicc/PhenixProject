::A cartridge is four parts and a name. The name tells you almost everything, if you know how to read it, and it's what prevents the accident.::

## What's in a cartridge

**The case**, usually brass, sometimes lacquered steel or aluminium. It holds everything together, and when fired it expands against the chamber to seal it.

**The primer**, in the base. A small shock-sensitive charge, struck by the firing pin, that sends a flame into the powder.

**The powder**, today smokeless, based on nitrocellulose. It doesn't detonate: it burns very fast and makes gas that pushes.

**The projectile**, the bullet. Bare lead, copper-jacketed lead, solid copper, sometimes with a steel core.

Two big families of priming:

- **Rimfire**: the priming compound sits in the rim of the base. The firing pin crushes the edge. That's the .22 LR. These cartridges can't be reloaded.
- **Centerfire**: a round primer in the centre of the base. Almost all the others. The case can be reloaded.

## Reading a caliber name

**The metric system** gives two numbers: bullet diameter, then case length, in millimetres. 9 × 19 mm, 7.62 × 39 mm, 5.56 × 45 mm.

**The imperial system** gives the diameter in hundredths or thousandths of an inch, followed by a name: .22 Long Rifle, .45 ACP, .308 Winchester.

**The same round often has several names.** 9 × 19 mm is also called 9 mm Parabellum, 9 mm Luger or 9 mm NATO. The .380 ACP is the 9 × 17 mm, also called 9 mm Short or 9 mm Kurz.

**And two different rounds sometimes share a number.** The 9 mm Makarov (9 × 18 mm) is not a 9 × 19. The 7.62 × 39 (Kalashnikov) has nothing to do with the 7.62 × 51 (NATO) or the 7.62 × 54R (Mosin-Nagant, Dragunov). **Always read the full marking, never just the number.**

The marking is stamped on the case base (the headstamp): maker, caliber, sometimes year. On the gun, the caliber is engraved on the barrel or the slide. **The two must match exactly.**

## The shotgun: caliber backwards

For smoothbore guns, the "gauge" is a number of lead balls: a 12 gauge barrel has the diameter of a lead ball weighing one twelfth of a pound. **The bigger the number, the smaller the barrel.** A 12 gauge is about 18.5 mm, a 20 gauge about 15.6 mm. The .410 is the exception: it's a true diameter in inches.

Three main loads:

- **Birdshot** (sizes 6 to 9): birds, small game. It loses its energy within a few dozen metres.
- **Buckshot**: about ten large pellets. Big game at short range, defence.
- **Slug**: a single heavy projectile, for big game.

Shell length matters too: 65, 70 or 76 mm (2½, 2¾ or 3 in). A shell too long for the chamber raises pressure dangerously.

## Bullet types

- **Full metal jacket (FMJ)**: lead core fully covered in copper. It goes through and deforms little. Standard military round.
- **Jacketed hollow point (JHP)**: a cavity at the front. It opens on impact, transfers its energy, goes through less. Police and defence round.
- **Soft point (SP)**: exposed lead at the front that flattens in a controlled way. The classic hunting round.
- **Tracer**: a compound in the base burns in flight and shows the trajectory. It can start a fire in dry grass.
- **Armour-piercing**: hard steel or tungsten core, to go through protection.
- **Subsonic**: under 340 m/s, so no supersonic crack. Useful with a suppressor.

## What can't be swapped

**A cartridge is only fired in the gun made for it.** The exceptions are known and limited:

- a .357 Magnum revolver fires .38 Special, not the reverse;
- a gun chambered in 5.56 NATO fires .223 Remington; the reverse is not advised, 5.56 runs at higher pressure;
- a gun in 7.62 × 51 generally fires .308 Winchester.

Everything else is a bet on the strength of the gun and your hands. **A 9 × 18 in a 9 × 19 gun can fire badly; a 7.62 × 39 won't fit a 7.62 × 51 and the other way round.**

## Tips from people who've handled it

- **The base doesn't lie.** A primer struck but not fired (a hang fire): keep the gun pointed in a safe direction for at least 30 seconds before opening. The round can still go off.
- **A green primer or a case pitted with verdigris**: don't fire it, it has taken on moisture.
- **Eastern-bloc military ammunition** often has corrosive primers (potassium chlorate): after shooting, clean the barrel with hot water, or it rusts within days.
- **Storage**: cool, dry, away from temperature swings. A military metal ammo can with a seal, and a desiccant sachet. Well stored, modern ammunition lasts decades. See [[SURV-MUN-001]].
- **Weight**: a thousand rounds of 9 mm weigh about 12 kg (26 lb); of .22 LR, about 3.5 kg (8 lb). That's why the .22 LR remains the most practical trade good of its kind.
- **A cartridge thrown in a fire** isn't a fired bullet: without a barrel, the case bursts and throws fragments, but the bullet leaves at low speed. Dangerous for the eyes, rarely more.

## The law

In France, ammunition follows the category of the gun: buying it generally requires the same permit as the gun (shooting licence, hunting licence, authorisation). Rules vary widely from country to country. A gun or ammunition you find is reported to the police: see [[SURV-ARM-001]].
