::A street light decides nothing. A sensor tells it night has fallen, a cabinet on the corner sends it power, and someone at the town hall chose the hour it switches off.::

## How a street lights up

**The control cabinet.** Street lights aren't wired to the grid one by one. They form circuits of a few dozen lights, fed by a grey metal **cabinet**, often at the foot of a wall or next to a transformer. It holds the protection, the meter and the switching.

**Switching** turns the whole circuit on and off at once:

- either by a **photocell**, a small light sensor on the cabinet or on a lamp post, which switches on when daylight drops below a threshold;
- or by an **astronomical clock**, which works out sunset and sunrise for the town every day;
- more and more, by **remote management**: each light gets its orders remotely and can dim in the middle of the night.

**Night switch-off.** Thousands of French towns turn off part of their lighting in the dead of night, often between midnight and 5 am, to save energy and limit light pollution. The law also governs the direction and colour of the light.

## The pole

A lamp post stands **4 to 12 metres** (13 to 40 ft) tall depending on the road: low in pedestrian streets, tall on highways. The column is a hollow tube of galvanised steel or aluminium. **At the base, a small hatch** gives access to the connection box and the fuse for that light.

## Lamps, from oldest to newest

| Lamp | Light | What to know |
|---|---|---|
| Gas | Soft yellow | City lighting in the 19th century. A lamplighter came by every evening with his pole. |
| Mercury vapour | Bluish white | Banned from sale in the European Union since 2015, still in place in forgotten corners. |
| Low-pressure sodium | Pure orange | Very efficient, but a single colour: under it, you can't tell red from green. |
| High-pressure sodium | Golden orange | The light of roads and cities for forty years. Takes several minutes to reach full brightness. |
| Metal halide | White | Squares, stadiums, monuments. |
| LED | White, warmer or cooler | The standard today. Half the energy of sodium, instant on, dimmable. |

## The solar street light

In car parks, on cycle paths and at isolated stops, more and more street lights are **self-contained**: a solar panel at the top of the pole, a battery (often lithium iron phosphate) in the pole or under the panel, a charge controller, an LED head and a motion sensor. No cable connects them to the grid. See [[TEC-SOLR-001]].

## What the maintenance crews know

- **Orange light hides colours.** Under sodium lighting, a map, colour-coded wiring or a wound is hard to read. A white head torch changes everything.
- **During a general blackout, solar street lights stay on.** They're often the only light in a neighbourhood. And each is a complete, self-contained kit: panel, battery, controller, LED light, which once taken down can light a room or charge a small battery. See [[SURV-ENE-001]].
- **The hatch at the base of the pole is live.** The supply cable is connected there at 230 volts, sometimes more. A hatch torn off after a crash gets reported, not explored.
- **Cover a photocell** and it thinks it's night: that's how technicians test lights in broad daylight. A photocell covered in bird droppings or badly aimed explains a street lit at noon.
- **A street light that blinks** at the end of its life is almost always a sodium lamp that switches off as it heats up and comes back on as it cools.
- **Lighting attracts insects**, especially under white and blue light. That's one reason for moving to warmer, orange-tinted LEDs near natural areas.
