::What kills after a head injury almost never happens at the moment of impact. It happens in the hours after, while everyone thinks it's over.::

## ACT

1. **Don't move the person** until you've ruled out a neck injury. Any blow hard enough to hurt the head can have hurt the spine: keep the head in line with the body.
2. **Check consciousness and breathing.** Unconscious: [[URG-INCO-001]], keeping the head, neck and trunk in line while turning them onto their side.
3. **Stop scalp bleeding** with pressure. It bleeds a lot and looks alarming, but it's rarely the main problem — see [[URG-HEMO-001]].
4. **Note the time of the blow and what happened.** Height of the fall, speed, whether they wore a helmet, any loss of consciousness and how long it lasted.
5. **Ask three simple questions**: where are we, what day is it, what happened. Repeat every half hour. **It's the comparison between two checks that tells you something, not a single check.**
6. **Keep the person resting, lying down, head slightly raised**, and don't leave them alone.
7. **Give nothing to eat or drink**, in case they need an operation.

**Get help without waiting if any of these signs appear:**

- loss of consciousness, even brief
- repeated vomiting
- increasing drowsiness, difficulty waking the person
- confusion, incoherent speech, unusual behaviour
- a headache that gets worse instead of better
- one pupil larger than the other
- weakness or tingling in a limb
- a seizure
- blood or clear fluid coming from the ear or nose
- double vision, problems with balance or speech

## ADAPT

**There are no signs and the person seems fine.** It's the most common case, and it doesn't excuse you from watching. **Twenty-four hours of close watch** with someone nearby, including at night. There's no need to keep them awake, but you must be able to wake them and check that they respond normally.

**It's a child.** The thresholds for worry are lower. A child who cries, asks for things and plays is reassuring. A child who is unusually quiet, hard to wake, or vomits repeatedly is an emergency.

**It's an elderly person, or someone on blood thinners.** The risk of bleeding inside the skull is clearly higher, and it can show up several days after a seemingly minor blow. Any head injury in this situation calls for medical advice, even without symptoms.

**The person has come round and wants to carry on.** It's common, and it should be refused. A second blow before full recovery is far more serious than the first. **At the slightest doubt, they stop for the day.**

**You're far from any help.** You can't treat anything, only watch and move them. What changes the decision: any sign from the list means you start the evacuation, even at night — see [[SIG-COM-001]].

**There's an open wound on the skull or a dent you can feel.** Don't press directly on it, don't remove anything, cover it without pressing and get help.

## UNDERSTAND

### Why things get worse later

The brain is enclosed in a rigid box with no spare room. A blow can tear a blood vessel; blood then slowly builds up between the skull and the brain, and **there's nowhere for it to go**. The pressure gradually rises and squeezes the brain.

This process takes time — sometimes a few hours, sometimes much longer in an elderly person or someone on blood thinners. That's what produces the typical pattern: a blow, a period where everything seems normal, then a decline.

**That's exactly why the watching matters.** Without it, the decline is discovered too late.

### What the pupils tell you

A pupil that widens on one side only points to pressure on the nerve on that side, and so to rising pressure. It's a late and serious sign. Its absence proves nothing; its presence calls for immediate evacuation.

### Concussion

A blow can disturb how the brain works without any visible injury: brief confusion, no memory of the moments before the blow, headache, sensitivity to light, slowness. Recovery takes rest, physical and mental, and comes gradually.

The key point is this: **the vulnerability lasts throughout recovery**. A second injury during this period has consequences out of all proportion to its own force. That's why nobody goes back to a risky activity until every sign has gone.

### Helmets

A helmet doesn't prevent the injury; it reduces the energy passed on and prevents a fracture. That's already a lot. A helmet that has taken a blow has absorbed energy by deforming, sometimes invisibly: it no longer protects and must be replaced.
