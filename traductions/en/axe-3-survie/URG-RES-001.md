::Three moves stop most disasters at home: close a valve, flip a switch, turn a tap. As long as you know where they are before you need them.::

## ACT

**You smell gas.**

1. **Don't touch any switch**, not to turn on, not to turn off. No flame, no doorbell, no phone call inside.
2. **Open the windows** on your way, without lingering.
3. **Close the gas valve** if it's on your way out: near the meter, a quarter turn. **Handle across the pipe: closed.**
4. **Get everyone out**, and leave the door open.
5. **Call from outside**, away from the house: the gas emergency number, or the fire service.

**Water is pouring and won't stop.** Close the main stop valve, often near the water meter: basement, under the sink, a utility cupboard, or a box in the ground outside. Turn it **clockwise**. Then cut the power if the water is reaching sockets or the fuse box.

**You need to cut the power.** At the fuse box, flip the main switch or the main breaker, often near the meter. **Dry hands, dry feet.** If the box is already in water: don't go near it, get out and call for help.

**First of all, today:** find all three. Label them, leave an adjustable wrench next to the gas valve if it needs one, and write down where they are in a sheet of your own, with a photo.

## ADAPT

**After shutting off the gas**, don't turn it back on yourself. Every appliance has to be checked and relit: that's a professional's job, and the rule in many countries.

**Earthquake.** Only shut off the gas if you smell it or hear it hiss. Shutting it off "just in case" leaves you without heating until a technician comes by, and they'll have a thousand other houses to see.

**A flood is coming.** Cut the power **before** the water gets in, while the fuse box is still dry. Then turn nothing back on until an electrician has looked at the installation.

**Fire.** Cut the power if it's on your way out, never by going back in. See [[URG-INC-001]].

**The water valve is stuck.** A valve that's never been turned seizes up with limescale. Work it gently, with small back-and-forth moves, and no extension on the wrench: an old valve that snaps is a leak you can no longer stop. Last resort: the valve on the street side of the meter, or the water company.

**You're draining the pipes or the hot water tank.** Cut the power to the water heater first: an electric element running dry burns out in minutes.

**You live in a flat.** The meter and the gas valve are often on the landing or in a service duct, sometimes shared by the whole building. Find the building's ones too, and who has the key.

**Turning the power back on.** Switch the main switch back on, then the breakers one by one. If one trips straight away, leave it off: the fault is on that circuit.

## UNDERSTAND

**Why no switch.** A switch makes a tiny spark every time its contacts open or close, even when you turn something off. Natural gas ignites when it makes up roughly 5 to 15 % of the air: a room that smells strongly can be right in that range. A doorbell, a phone, a fridge motor kicking in make the same spark.

**Why it smells.** Natural gas has no smell at all. A sulphur compound that smells of rotten eggs is added on purpose, so a leak gets noticed at levels far below the ones that explode.

**Why a quarter turn.** A gas valve is a ball with a hole through it. The handle always lines up with the hole: along the pipe, gas flows; across it, gas is blocked. No label to read, no light needed.

**Why water and power go together.** Water conducts electricity. A fuse box or a power strip sitting in water can make all the water in the room live, with nothing to see. That's why you cut the power before stepping into a flooded basement, and never while standing in the water.

**Why find them now.** A burst pipe can release tens of litres a minute. By the time you've found the valve with a torch, the ceiling below has come down. The three places fit in three lines: it's the shortest and most useful sheet you'll ever write.
