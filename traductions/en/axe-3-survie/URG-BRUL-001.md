::Cool running water for twenty minutes does more for a burn than anything you could put on it.::

## ACT

1. **Remove the cause.** Get away from the source, and put out flames by laying the person down and smothering the fire. Someone who runs feeds their own flames.
2. **Cool with lukewarm water, between fifteen and twenty-five degrees (59 to 77 °F), for twenty minutes.** No ice, no ice-cold water: extreme cold worsens the injury and drops the body's temperature. Twenty minutes is a duration, not an estimate — count it.
3. **Take off rings, watches, bracelets and belts** before the swelling traps them. It's urgent, and it's always forgotten.
4. **Don't pull off clothing stuck to the skin.** Cut around it.
5. **Cover loosely** with a clean, lint-free cloth. Cling film laid flat, not wrapped around, protects well and doesn't stick.
6. **Keep the rest of the body warm** while you cool the burned area. A large burn cooled on an undressed person outdoors leads to [[URG-HYPO-001]] within half an hour.

**Apply nothing else.** No butter, no oil, no toothpaste, no honey, no plants. Anything greasy traps heat in the tissue and turns a surface burn into a deep one.

**Never pop a blister.** It's a sterile, natural dressing. Once open, it becomes a way in for infection.

## ADAPT

**You don't have twenty minutes of water.** Cool what you can, as early as you can. The benefit is greatest in the first three minutes and remains real for up to three hours afterwards. A wet cloth changed constantly beats a wet cloth left in place, which warms up and stops cooling anything.

**It's a drowning.** See [[URG-SEC-002]]: the resuscitation sequence is different there.

**The product was swallowed or inhaled.** See [[URG-SEC-003]]: don't induce vomiting.

**It's a chemical burn.** Rinse generously and for a long time, much longer than for a heat burn. Remove soaked clothing without pulling it over the head. Don't try to neutralise an acid with a base or the other way round: the reaction produces heat and makes everything worse.

**It's an electrical burn.** Cut the power before touching the person. What you see on the skin says nothing about the real extent: the current burned along its whole path inside the body. A small entry and exit wound can hide major damage. See [[TEC-ENE-001]] for how to cut the power safely.

**The burn is large, deep, or on the face, hands, joints or genitals.** These are the cases where getting the person out comes before everything else. Cool, cover, and call for help: see [[SIG-COM-001]].

**You're far from everything.** What you can do comes down to three things: keep the wound covered and clean, give small, regular sips to drink if the person is conscious — safe water only, see [[URG-EAU-001]] — and watch for signs of infection over the following days, meaning smell, heat, swelling and fever.

## UNDERSTAND

A burn doesn't stop when the source is gone. The stored heat keeps destroying tissue deeper down for several minutes: this is called burn progression. **Cooling isn't about easing the pain, it's about stopping the burn that's still going on.** That's why duration matters more than how cold the water is.

Severity is judged on two things, and the second matters most.

**Depth.** A painful redness that turns white when pressed is superficial and heals on its own. A blister with sharp pain reaches the dermis. Skin that is white, brown or leathery, and above all **painless**, is a deep burn: the nerve endings have been destroyed. No pain is a sign of severity, never good news.

**Size.** This is what kills, not depth. Skin holds in the body's water; destroyed over a large area, it lets a huge volume of fluid escape, and the person dies of dehydration and shock long before dying of the wound. For a quick estimate, the victim's palm, fingers included, is about one percent of their body surface.

That's also why a burn on a child is more serious than the same burn on an adult: for the same area, the share of the body affected is far greater.

One last point, often fatal and invisible. **Someone burned in an enclosed space may have burned their airways.** Soot around the nostrils, a hoarse voice, coughing, singed nose hair: these are signs of internal damage that will swell over the following hours. There's nothing to do about it in the field, but you need to know it so a clean-looking skin doesn't reassure you.
