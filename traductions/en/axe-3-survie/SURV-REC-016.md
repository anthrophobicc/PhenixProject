::Every wreck — car, truck, farm machine, boat, plane — contains the same families of materials. Know those families, and you know what to look for on any carcass, without needing to know the model.::

## UNDERSTAND

### Materials common to every wreck

Every vehicle or machine combines the same basic components, in different proportions and qualities. The specific sheets ([[SURV-REC-004]] to [[SURV-REC-011]]) cover wrecks by type. This one covers what you'll find everywhere.

## ACT

### Metals

**Steel.** The main material of every wreck. Chassis, body, bolts, springs, driveshafts. Not all steel is equal: body steel is soft and easy to work; spring steel is hard, elastic and tough — it's one of the best salvageable steels for making tools and blades. See [[TEC-MEC-005]].

**Copper.** In all the electrical wiring, motors, alternators, old radiators. Copper melts at about 1,085 °C and can be recycled forever without losing its properties. A car alternator holds several hundred grams of copper in its windings. See [[TEC-ENE-001]].

**Aluminum.** Wheels, engine casings, modern radiators, recent bodywork, aircraft parts. It melts at about 660 °C — castable in a forge fire. Light, corrosion-resistant, easy to work. See [[TEC-COR-001]].

**Lead.** Lead-acid batteries (every car battery holds five to ten kilos), counterweights, old solder. Toxic — never melt it in an enclosed space, and wash your hands after handling it. It melts at 327 °C and is easy to cast into sling shot, fishing weights or ballast.

### Ball bearings

Found in every rotating part: wheels, motors, alternators, pumps, driveshafts. Hardened steel balls in a steel or brass cage. Bearings are precision objects, hard to make and easy to salvage. Uses: replacing a worn bearing, balls as slingshot ammunition, balls as high-grade steel for tools.

### Springs

Suspension springs (big, high-strength steel), valve springs (small, very hard), return springs (everywhere in mechanisms). Spring metal is a high-carbon steel, hardened and tempered — it's the best material for making blades, punches and needles. It can be worked in a forge. See [[TEC-MEC-005]].

### Glass

**Windshields.** Laminated glass (two layers of glass with a plastic film between them). It doesn't shatter into sharp pieces — it cracks and stays in place. Laminated glass can be cut and reused as greenhouse glazing, a protective panel or a flat surface.

**Side windows.** Tempered glass. It breaks into thousands of small, blunt cubes. Useless as a pane, but the cubes make a good abrasive.

**Mirrors.** A reflective surface: signaling (see [[SIG-COM-001]]), concentrating sunlight, or a rear-view mirror reused as is.

### Rubber

Tires, seals, hoses, rubber mounts, belts. Rubber isn't easy to recycle by hand. Tires can be cut with a knife or a saw and used as soles, washers, gaskets, protection, or fuel of last resort (toxic black smoke — outdoors only). Hoses are flexible pipes you can use to transfer liquids.

### Fluids

**Engine oil.** Lubrication, waterproofing wood and leather, an oil lamp (a cloth wick, a container, a flame — see [[SURV-LUM-001]]).

**Coolant.** Water plus antifreeze (ethylene glycol, toxic). Antifreeze is a solvent, a de-icer and a violent poison — never drink it, and never mistake it for water.

**Leftover fuel.** Gasoline, diesel, kerosene. An "empty" tank often still holds several liters. Diesel keeps better than gasoline and works as fuel, solvent and degreaser.

**Brake fluid.** It absorbs water, it's flammable, and it eats paint. Usable as a fire accelerant in controlled amounts.

### Electronics and electrics

**Alternator or generator.** It produces current when you turn it. It's a generator you can salvage as is, with a belt and an engine. See [[TEC-ENE-001]].

**Starter motor.** A powerful electric motor, powered by the battery. Reusable as a motor (winch, lathe, pump) with a suitable power supply.

**Wiring harness.** Dozens of meters of insulated copper wire, sized by cross-section. Salvageable for any wiring job.

## ADAPT

**The wreck is at sea or in water.** Salt corrodes steel quickly, but aluminum and copper hold up better. Rinse salvaged parts in fresh water. Oil and fuel float and can be collected from the surface.

**The wreck burned.** Metals survive (warped but salvageable), glass melts or shatters, rubber and plastic are gone. Ball bearings and springs lose their temper (heat destroys the heat treatment) — they become ordinary steel.

**You don't know what a fluid is.** The label, where it sits in the vehicle and its color tell you. Engine oil: black or amber, in the oil pan at the bottom. Coolant: green, pink or orange, in the radiator's closed circuit. Brake fluid: clear, in a small reservoir on the master cylinder. When in doubt, don't taste it and don't breathe it in.

## What to remember

**Springs and bearings are the hidden treasures** of any wreck: top-quality steel and mechanical precision you can't find anywhere else.

**Copper is everywhere** — wiring, motors, alternators — and recycles without loss. Look for it systematically.

**Fluids are both resources and dangers.** Oil lubricates and gives light, antifreeze kills, fuel burns. Identify them before you handle them.
